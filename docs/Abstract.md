## Abstract

Organisations collect far more customer feedback than they can read, yet methods such as lexicon scoring, classical machine learning, transformer models and retrieval-augmented generation are often selected on reputation rather than evidence. Existing comparative studies are commonly based on single datasets and aggregate metrics, which can conceal how methods behave across different types of feedback and where they fail.

This dissertation compares these approaches stage by stage using a corpus of **642,692 reviews** spanning Amazon products, Yelp businesses and airline complaint tweets, within a common comparative framework. Five sentiment classifiers were evaluated using per-class, per-platform and confusion-matrix analysis alongside McNemar significance testing. BERTopic was used to discover complaint topics separately by platform before consolidation into a **24-category cross-domain taxonomy**, assessed without ground truth through coherence, coverage and distribution measures. A retrieval-augmented question-answering pipeline over a FAISS index was also compared with a standalone language model using four RAGAS metrics.

The central finding is that method ranking depends on domain. Fine-tuned DistilBERT achieved the highest clean held-out macro F1 of **0.8375**, while pretrained RoBERTa scored **0.7853 on airline tweets** but only **0.5544 on Yelp**, outperforming both classical models on the platform closest to its training domain while underperforming logistic regression on the pooled evaluation. Diagnostic experiments showed that this behaviour was concentrated in the neutral class and was consistent with a mismatch between label definitions rather than general model weakness. Fine-tuning's clearest benefit was cross-platform stability rather than peak accuracy. The taxonomy produced well-separated categories but left **27.6% of reviews unclassified** and assigned some positive reviews to complaint categories. Retrieval augmentation achieved **0.540 faithfulness** under a hard grounding threshold added to the retriever, a property not defined for the ungrounded baseline, while scoring lower on mean answer relevancy (0.610 against 0.753). Component-level metrics located retrieval, rather than generation, as the main measured weakness of the pipeline: on the question where retrieval itself was weakest, faithfulness fell with it rather than staying artificially high, evidence that the threshold suppresses confident answers built on weak evidence rather than masking the weakness. A separate, executed ablation on metadata tagging found a genuine trade-off rather than a clean validation — faithfulness improved by 0.118 and answer relevancy fell by 0.202 — alongside one directly observed instance of a hallucinated platform citation that tagging prevented.

The study contributes a stage-wise cross-domain comparison, diagnostic analysis of anomalous results, and evidence showing how aggregate metrics can conceal important failure modes. The generation evaluation was limited to **five questions judged by the generating model**, and of four designed RAG ablations, one (metadata tagging) was built and executed while the other three (retrieval depth, retriever type, prompt version) were not carried out, so conclusions at this stage remain directional rather than definitive.

**Keywords:** sentiment analysis, retrieval-augmented generation, topic modelling, cross-domain evaluation, customer feedback analytics, transformer models
